L06 · Pygame Practice

🎯 Eight ways to play with Pygame

Small, gentle exercises to build your Pygame confidence — one window, one shape, one idea at a time.

CourseYear 9 Game On
LessonL06
Time30–45 min
MarksNone — practice only

If you can type the five-line Pygame skeleton and see a window appear, you already know more than you think. These eight exercises grow gently — each one adds one small new idea on top of the last.

There are no marks. No submit button. The point is to feel what Pygame does — to build a small picture, watch the loop tick, make a shape move when you press a key. Confidence comes from many small wins, not one large one.

Work through them in order if you can. Use the starter-L06-pygame.py file from the lesson page as your starting point. If you finish in class, keep going at home. If you get stuck for more than five minutes, raise your hand — there is no penalty for asking.

Warm-up · exercises 1–3
EX 01

Make the window yours

★ · easy ≈ 3 minutes

Open your starter-L06-pygame.py file and change three things: the window size, the title, and the background colour. The point is to feel the file responding to your edits.

# Change these three lines in starter-L06-pygame.py:

screen = pygame.display.set_mode((1200, 800))        # 1. size
pygame.display.set_caption("Alien Invasion")         # 2. title
bg_color = (230, 230, 230)                           # 3. colour
What you'll seeA window of your chosen size, with your chosen title in the top bar, painted in your chosen colour. Run it. Close it. Change something else. Run it again.
Why this mattersPygame programs are small files you edit and re-run. The faster you get used to the loop of edit → save → run → see → edit again, the easier every following lesson becomes.
EX 02

Draw a single rectangle

★ · easy ≈ 4 minutes

Add one rectangle to your window. Position it anywhere you like. Choose a colour that contrasts with your background so you can see it clearly.

# Add this INSIDE the while loop,
# AFTER screen.fill() but BEFORE pygame.display.flip():

pygame.draw.rect(
    screen,
    (200, 50, 50),         # colour — red-ish
    (100, 100, 150, 80)    # (x, y, width, height)
)
What you'll seeA solid rectangle sitting on your background. Try changing each number in the position tuple to see how the rectangle moves and resizes.
Try thisMove the rectangle to the centre of your window. If your window is 800×600, the centre is roughly (350, 270, 100, 60). The maths gets easier with practice.
EX 03

Add a circle next to it

★ · easy ≈ 4 minutes

Now add a circle. Both shapes should appear on the screen at the same time. Pygame draws them in the order you list them in your code.

pygame.draw.rect(
    screen, (200, 50, 50),
    (100, 100, 150, 80)
)
pygame.draw.circle(
    screen,
    (50, 100, 200),         # colour — blue
    (400, 200),             # centre (x, y)
    50                      # radius in pixels
)
What you'll seeA red rectangle and a blue circle, both visible at the same time. They live happily side by side.
Why this mattersEvery Pygame frame is built by drawing shapes (or images) in order, one on top of the next. Once you can place two shapes, you can place two hundred — the pattern is the same.
Building scenes · exercises 4–5
EX 04

Build a tiny picture

★★ · gentle stretch ≈ 6 minutes

Combine rectangles and circles to make something recognisable. A house, a face, a robot, a flag, a traffic light. The picture does not have to be art — it has to be made entirely from shapes.

# Example: a simple house

# Body of the house (brown rectangle)
pygame.draw.rect(screen, (160, 120, 80), (200, 250, 200, 200))

# Roof (a triangle is three lines, but we'll cheat with a polygon)
pygame.draw.polygon(screen, (140, 60, 60),
                    [(200, 250), (400, 250), (300, 150)])

# Door
pygame.draw.rect(screen, (90, 50, 30), (275, 350, 50, 100))

# Window (small blue square)
pygame.draw.rect(screen, (200, 220, 255), (230, 290, 40, 40))
What you'll seeYour chosen picture, built from coloured shapes. It will not look photo-real — that is fine. It is yours.
Try thisPhotograph or screenshot your picture and post it on the L06 Google Classroom thread. Seeing thirty different pictures made from the same primitives is one of the best parts of teaching this lesson.
EX 05

Draw a row of circles with a loop

★★ · gentle stretch ≈ 6 minutes

Use a for loop to draw ten circles in a row. Each one should be a little to the right of the last. This is the first hint at how Pygame draws fleets of aliens later in the term.

# Inside the while loop, after screen.fill():

for i in range(10):
    x = 50 + i * 60      # 50, 110, 170, 230, ...
    y = 300
    pygame.draw.circle(screen, (100, 150, 200), (x, y), 20)
What you'll seeA horizontal row of ten blue circles, evenly spaced. Each one is 60 pixels to the right of the previous one.
Why this mattersIn a few lessons you will use almost exactly this code to spawn a fleet of aliens. The aliens will be images instead of plain circles, and there will be rows as well as columns — but the loop pattern is the same.
Try thisMake a grid. Use two nested for loops — one for the row, one for the column — to draw a 5×4 grid of circles. This is genuinely how Matthes builds the alien fleet in Chapter 13.
Moving and responding · exercises 6–7
EX 06

Make a shape move on its own

★★★ · stretch ≈ 8 minutes

Animate a rectangle so that it slides across the screen by itself. Use a variable to remember the rectangle's x position, and add a little to it every frame.

# Before the while loop:
rect_x = 0
rect_speed = 2

# Inside the while loop, after screen.fill():
rect_x += rect_speed                       # move a bit each frame
pygame.draw.rect(screen, (200, 100, 0),
                 (rect_x, 250, 80, 50))    # draw at new position
What you'll seeAn orange rectangle gliding from left to right across your window. It will eventually slide off the right edge and disappear.
Try thisMake the rectangle bounce. When rect_x goes past the right edge of the screen, reverse its direction by setting rect_speed = -rect_speed. Do the same on the left. You have just written your first piece of game logic.
Why this mattersAll animation in games is a variable, changed slightly every frame, then redrawn. Once you can move one rectangle, you can move a ship, a bullet, an alien, or a cloud across the sky. The pattern is the same.
EX 07

Respond to a key press

★★★ · stretch ≈ 8 minutes

Make your rectangle move only when the right arrow key is held down. This is a small change to your event loop — but it is the start of every controllable character in every game you will ever write.

# Inside the event loop, after the QUIT check:

keys = pygame.key.get_pressed()
if keys[pygame.K_RIGHT]:
    rect_x += 4
if keys[pygame.K_LEFT]:
    rect_x -= 4
What you'll seeA rectangle that sits still — until you press the right arrow, when it slides right. Press left to slide left. Release both and it stops.
Try thisAdd up and down. Use K_UP and K_DOWN with a separate rect_y variable. Now you have a four-direction controllable square. This is essentially how Pac-Man moves.
Why this mattersThe whole reason we use a game loop is to keep checking — every frame — what the player is doing. Once you can read the keyboard, you can wire keys to anything: jump, shoot, pause, restart. The hard part is over.
Your turn · exercise 8
EX 08

Build something small that is yours

★★★★ · open ≈ 10 minutes or more

Combine any of the ideas you have practised above into one short Pygame program that you wanted to write. There is no specification. There is no rubric. Make something you would want to show a friend.

Some starting ideas, in case the blank page is intimidating:

  • A street scene with houses, a car that drives across, and a sun in the sky.
  • A bouncing ball that changes colour each time it hits the wall.
  • A tiny dot you can move around with the arrow keys, leaving a trail behind it.
  • A traffic light that switches colour every two seconds (use a counter and modulo arithmetic).
  • A field of stars (small white circles at random positions) on a dark sky.
  • A loading bar that fills up over five seconds and then stops.
If you want feedbackSave your program as practice_yourname.py and email it to Ms Gao, or post a screenshot in the L06 Classroom thread. There are no marks. There is only the satisfaction of seeing what you made.
Why this mattersEverything you have done in exercises 1–7 has been someone else's instruction. Exercise 8 is the first time the program is yours. The act of inventing a tiny project — even one that is silly — is how programmers grow.

If you finished all eight exercises in one sitting, you are ahead. If you finished three or four, you are exactly on track. If you finished one, you have still learned something. Pygame rewards patience more than speed.

The next lesson, L07, adds a ship image to your window — your first sprite. Everything you have practised here will carry forward. The shapes become images, the rectangle becomes a player, and the key-press logic becomes movement. The pattern is already in your hands.
